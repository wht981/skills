# Structure Models

Choose a structure that makes the knowledge relationships visible in the note layout. Identify the dominant relationship first, then select one primary structure.

| Structure | Dominant relationship | Selection signal | Typical note form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tower | Support, constraint, premise, and conclusion | Higher layers depend on lower layers, or hard constraints define what is possible | Layers from foundations to applications, with higher layers linking downward |
| Tree | Whole and part, category and subtype | Clear classification is needed, with emphasis on coverage and separation | Directory hierarchy, nested lists, or layered sections |
| Flow | Predecessor and successor, state transition | Order carries meaning or the process has a time direction | Numbered notes or strictly ordered sections |
| Network | Mutual explanation and multidirectional association | Concepts provide context for one another without one stable hierarchy | Flat notes, a small number of hubs, and cross-links |
| Spiral | Return and progressive deepening | The same subject must be revisited at increasing levels of depth | Notes grouped by pass, with each pass linking to the previous one |
| Matrix | Intersection of two independent dimensions | One dimension cannot support comparison, selection, or placement | Group by one dimension and express the other through tables, tags, or links |
| Onion | Stable core and replaceable periphery | The core mechanism is stable while outer implementations or strategies can change | Move from the core outward, grouping by stability |

## Selection sequence

1. Determine whether hard constraints, complete classification, irreversible process, or mutual definition dominates the content. Prefer tower, tree, flow, or network respectively.
2. Determine whether the content requires two-dimensional comparison, core-versus-periphery layering, or repeated deepening. Consider matrix, onion, or spiral respectively.
3. When several structures remain plausible, use the user's intent to decide. Conditions of possibility favor a tower; operation favors an onion or flow; comparison favors a matrix or network; global orientation favors a tree or tower.

Choose one primary structure. Add a secondary structure only when an important relationship cannot be represented by the primary one. For example, a tree may determine directories while network links express lateral dependencies.

## Mismatch signals

Rebuild the structure when any of the following occurs:

- The same concept appears in three or more places without adding new relational information.
- Parent-child, layer, or sequence relationships cannot be named precisely in one sentence.
- The stable core, replaceable periphery, and scope boundary are mixed together.
- Preserving the form distorts the content or hides relationships the user actually needs.

A network hub may appear in many connections as long as each connection supplies different context.
